# Principles

Six rules for deciding things when the three adjectives are not specific enough. Each one has a cost attached, because a principle that costs nothing is a slogan.

## 1. Eight things, not eight hundred

If we cannot say why something is on the list, it is not on the list. Eight good things beats eight hundred sorted by popularity, because the user's problem is not a lack of options, it is not knowing which option is any good.

**Costs:** inventory, search-result breadth, and the ability to say "everything in Goa".

**In practice:** the default feed is a human-ordered shortlist, not a ranked index. Search exists but is not the primary surface. Growth does not come from adding listings, and a listings count is never a metric anyone celebrates.

## 2. We have been to everything on the list

Every listing carries a view from someone who went. That is the claim in the positioning and it is the whole basis of the brand, so it sets the ceiling on how fast the catalogue grows: we add listings at the speed we can visit them, not at the speed we can sign them.

A verdict written by someone who did not go is the same failure as an invented spots-left count, and it takes one bad recommendation for a user to discount every other one.

**Costs:** the list grows at walking pace, and a competitor will always have more listings than us. That comparison is one we lose on purpose.

**In practice:** launch checked-only. If aggregated inventory is ever added, the positioning sentence changes **before** the inventory does, and checked and unchecked become visibly different things.

## 3. The first price is the price

Whatever number a user sees first is what their card gets charged. Fees, taxes, everything, from the card in the feed onward.

**Costs:** our prices look higher than competitors who reveal fees at checkout. We will lose some comparisons on the listing page and win them at the payment screen.

**In practice:** PriceBreakdown forbids bundled lines, its total must match BookingBar exactly, and that match deserves an automated test.

## 4. Never manufacture urgency

A spots-left count appears only when the number is real and under five. No countdown timers, no "12 people are viewing this", no invented "was" prices.

**Costs:** measurable short-term conversion. This is the most expensive principle here and the one most likely to be quietly abandoned under pressure.

**In practice:** the honesty rule is already enforced in Badge, Banner, Rating and PriceBreakdown. If this principle goes, those four specs go with it, and someone should say so out loud rather than letting it erode ticket by ticket.

## 5. Speak like someone who lives here

Plain Indian English, specific place names, real durations. Not tourism-board English, not Silicon Valley English, and not borrowed slang.

**Costs:** we sound less "young" than competitors who use slang, and we cannot reuse generic tourism copy.

**In practice:** "Anjuna, 2 km away" not "conveniently located". "Speaks Konkani" not "local expert".

## 6. Boring beats clever when money is involved

Anywhere a user is paying, cancelling or reading a ticket, the copy gets duller and the layout gets plainer. Personality lives in discovery; it does not live in checkout.

**Costs:** the funnel is the least characterful part of the product, which will feel like a missed opportunity to someone.

**In practice:** the confirmation says when, where, and that it is paid. Errors say what happened, whether money moved, and what to do next, in that order.

## How to use these

When two options both look defensible, find the principle that rules one out. If none does, the decision is genuinely open and does not need a brand argument, just a choice.

When a principle blocks something valuable, that is the principle working. Change it deliberately, in this file, with the cost restated. Do not route around it in a single screen.
